Perceptual Learning

This study is part of a larger project designed to test what is called perceptual learning. Previous studies have shown that when people hear words from speakers who have slightly different speech patterns, they are often able to adjust their perception of speech sounds to match the shifted input. This process of accommodation may help explain how the sound patterns of language change over time.

To test this perceptual learning process further, we needed to create speech sounds that are slightly different from what people would normally hear. First, we prepared a set of sounds that were between an “s” sound and an “sh” sound by mixing original recordings of these two sounds together. Other participants then categorized whether they heard these sounds as “s” or “sh”. This allowed us to identify a maximally ambiguous blend: a sound that people can hear as either “s” or “sh”, depending on the context.

This ambiguous sound was then inserted into words that normally end in an “sh” sound.

In the experiment you just completed, you were first asked to identify whether sounds were “s” or “sh”. This gives us a measure of your baseline perception of these two sounds. Then, we presented the ambiguous sound described above in a spoken word identification task. We expect that hearing these sounds may shift your perception of the speaker’s “s” and “sh” sounds. We measure this change in the final categorization phase.

The amount that your perception shifts will be compared with results from other participants who completed different experimental conditions, such as conditions involving multiple speakers or different speech sounds.

Thank you for your participation in this project. Your responses will help us better understand the nature of speech perception.
